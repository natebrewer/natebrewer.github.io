Strain Wave Gearbox

Theory and Background

Strain Wave Gearing

Strain wave gearboxes are a type of gearbox that relies on the controlled continuous deformation of an internal spline to transmit power. As the diagram at the left shows, strain wave gearboxes rely on an external circular spline (blue), a deformable inner flexspline (red), and a wave generator which controls the deformation (green). Strain wave gearboxes are extremely useful for a wide range of applications, especially robotics, as they fit extremely high gear ratios into small, light form factors. In the same equivalent space to a 10:1 planetary gearbox, strain wave gearing can achieve a 320:1 ratio. The operating principle behind the power transfer is that there are two points of contact maintained with identical tooth profiles, but the inner flexspline has less teeth than the outer circular spline. This means that while the two mesh together at the contact point like regular gears, the flex spline rotates less per unit revolution of the wave generator by the tooth difference between the two. Therefore, the flexspline is driven backwards at a rate per revolution proportional to the tooth difference, leading to massive gear ratios.

I chose this project for a variety of reasons, but the main one is that I have always been interested in odd mechanical systems. The idea of a gearbox that intentionally bends and flexes and somehow achieves the greatest gear-ratio to volume seemed really interesting to me. I am also interested in robotics, where strain-wave gearboxes are frequently used due to that exact trade-off.

Mechanical Design

All of the CAD design on this project was done in SolidWorks. As stated earlier, the reduction of the gearbox is proportional to the tooth difference between the inner and outer splines. This design uses a 52 tooth outer spline with a 50 tooth flexspline, giving a reduction of 25:1 in this form factor. The wave generator is designed by adding the 2 times the gear module to the bore axis of the inner flexspline. This gives the bearing surface for the deformation, deforming by one tooth width in each direction along the major axis and providing the transfer points. My design utilizes a single roller bearing, but due to the ANSYS simulation results I may upgrade this to 3 per side to distribute the force more evenly.

Flexspline

The flexspline itself will be printed from 60A TPU, a more rigid form than the standard 95A durometer rating. This component has an extremely high factor of safety around 80, rating it well for sustained loading. The flexspline handles the power transmission to the output shaft, but is itself flexible and designed to deform. Therefore, I have designed a PLA cup to bolt onto the base of the flexspline. Since PLA is many times more rigid than TPU, this makes it much better suited to a power transmission interface. There are 6 M4 heat-set inserts in the output shaft, to allow attachment of many different couplers. The bearing set into this PLA insert provides the rigidity for the input shaft and wave generator.

Wave Generator

The wave generator is an extremely important part of the strain wave gearbox, as this is the component which deforms the flexspline to match with the circular spline. A generally elliptic arc is chosen, with a major axis equal to the inner bore diameter of the flexspline plus two times the gear module. This means that at the point of contact, the deformation is perfectly equal to the difference between splines, meshing the teeth. The minor axis is the bore diameter minus two times the gear module, ensuring that the teeth 90 degrees out of phase from each contact point are fully out of contact with the spline. I set my bearings tangent to this arc, ensuring that the point of contact lies on this constructed ellipse.

Finite Element Analysis

I wanted to try making the flexspline out of TPU rather than the PLA I had frequently seen, as PLA's brittleness makes it crack in a relatively short life cycle. To ensure that my TPU design could repeatedly withstand near constant deformation, I ran a finite-element simulation of it using ANSYS. I imported the relevant parts, then set the wave generator to rotate at about 60 RPM. I set the contact forces to ramp in, avoiding innaccurately large forces resulting from the initial deformation. I quickly ran into a problem though, which was modeling the correct material behavior. TPU behaves like a hyperelastic material, with large deformation, and I needed to know its exact material properties.

Material Properties

I searched around through research journals to find someone who may have characterized 60A TPU, and eventually found a paper by Lang et. al which had done exactly that. The team had investigated the structural properties of 3D printed TPU, even including data about the strength by orientation of the print. They had also collected this data for testing in ANSYS, meaning that I could use their exact results for my testing. I have linked their paper below by way of an unofficial citation, just because their work was so useful to my project. The material curves and constants are displayed adjacently.

Lang et. al (2025) These researchers model TPU as a polynomial, second-order hyperelastic material. Their computational results, and therefore my computational results, match physical testing to within 5% tolerance on force and displacement data.
